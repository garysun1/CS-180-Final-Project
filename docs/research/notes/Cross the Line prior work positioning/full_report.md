# Claim the crossing, credit everything underneath

Restyling a static scene once and putting the real people back on top is not new. Microsoft Teams Premium does it for webcam backgrounds, an open-source ComfyUI preset called FXjutsu "Toon World" does it for video in exactly Cross the Line's order (clean plate, restyle the empty plate, real people on top), and Layered Neural Atlases (2021) is the academic ancestor of "edit once, map to every frame." The research found no precedent in papers, products, social filters or art for the project's central experience: a spatial rule (a line, a depth threshold or a portal) that keeps one part of a single frame real and shows a pixel-aligned generated twin of the *same* scene elsewhere, with people changing world as they walk across. That crossing grammar, the foot-depth rule behind it, and the cheap generate-once design that lets it run live are what the team should champion. Everything beneath them should be credited plainly: the temporal-median plate, ControlNet, SDEdit's strength ladder, Brown–Lowe blending, homographies and Chuang-style ratio shadows. Most of those are CS180 course material, and naming them helps the grade. The project's own numbers back the story: **about 80 s per world on 4 CPU cores**, **a deliberately injected 3–4 px seam drift cut to 0.13–0.53 px by registration (and all 7 generated worlds needed no re-alignment at all)**, **35 dB PSNR** when people are composited back onto the clean plate, and **r = 0.80 between a person's height and the depth at their feet over 4,154 detections**. One hole matters most. The comparative claim that per-frame diffusion flickers and this method does not has no measured baseline yet, so it rests on other people's numbers until the team runs one. Every "nobody has done this" should become "we did not find," because TikTok, Instagram and festival archives were searched only partially.

## A tripod turns a neural atlas into a median

The cleanest academic framing is that Cross the Line occupies the degenerate case of atlas-based video editing. Layered Neural Atlases fit per-video coordinate networks that map every pixel to a 2D atlas, and an edit to the atlas is "mapped back to all frames," preserving "occlusions, deformation, shadows, and reflections" ([Kasten et al., arXiv 2109.11418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11418)). CoDeF retrieves its canonical image "by setting the deformation of all points to zero" ([arXiv 2308.07926](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.07926v2)). With a locked-off camera and a static scene, a background pixel sees the same scene point at every time step. The optimal background mapping is then constant in time, the atlas *is* the clean plate, and a per-pixel temporal median estimates it in closed form with no optimisation. No paper found states this explicitly. Generative Omnimatte comes closest when it criticises earlier work that "assume[s] a static background or accurate pose and depth" ([arXiv 2411.16683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16683)), so present the observation as the team's own derivation, not as a citation. It costs three things that atlases buy: moving cameras, editing the people's own layer, and learned alpha. The tripod also makes geometry degenerate but not photometry. Sun and clouds still change, and those changes ride in the frame/plate ratio, which is a hand-built cousin of Hashing-NVD's learned "multiplicative residual" for lighting ([CVF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2023/html/Chan_Hashing_Neural_Video_Decomposition_with_Multiplicative_Residuals_in_Space-Time_ICCV_2023_paper.html)).

The median itself is decades old. Piccardi's review credits Lo and Velastin with "the median value of the last n frames as the background model" and notes its known weaknesses: it needs a buffer and provides no deviation measure ([Piccardi 2004](https://zenodo.org/records/891536/files/article.pdf)). Its classic failure, anyone who stands still for more than half the clip being absorbed into the plate, belongs in the failure gallery. The "person = pixels that differ from the plate" rule is background subtraction, the same signal that Background Matting learns from when it asks for "an additional photo of the background without the subject" ([Sengupta et al., arXiv 2004.00626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00626)). It captures shadows and carried objects for free, which Omnimatte has to learn per video ([arXiv 2105.06993](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06993)). In VFX this is ordinary craft. Foundry's Nuke guide says "shooting a clean plate if the camera is locked off is easy" ([Foundry](https://learn.foundry.com/nuke/8.0/content/furnacecore/background.html)), and digital matte painting replaces environments behind rotoscoped actors. The pipeline automates four manual stages: clean plate, roto, matte painting and composite.

The per-frame literature explains why the plate matters. FRESCO's authors diagnose flicker precisely: attention-only guidance is a "soft constraint," flow guidance fails under occlusion and rapid motion, "frame-by-frame generation is only locally optimized," and inversion-free ControlNet methods are "prone to flickering" ([arXiv 2403.12962](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12962v1)). FreeViS adds that image-to-video models are "typically limited to about 81 frames" ([arXiv 2510.01686](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01686v1)), and MLV-Edit names "cumulative structural drift" and "boundary artifacts" when long video is processed in segments ([arXiv 2602.02123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02123)). All of these failures trace back to per-frame stochastic generation held together by approximate correspondence. For static pixels, Cross the Line never generates per frame, so there is no correspondence to get wrong. That is a statement about construction, not a measured win, until the baseline in the gap table exists.

The "what we started from" list is long, and saying it out loud earns rubric credit. The team started from SD 1.5, ControlNet depth and canny conditioning ([Zhang, Rao, Agrawala, ICCV 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2023/html/Zhang_Adding_Conditional_Control_to_Text-to-Image_Diffusion_Models_ICCV_2023_paper.html)), LCM-LoRA, Depth Anything V2, the temporal median, homographies, two-band blending and SDEdit's strength trade-off ([Meng et al., arXiv 2108.01073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.01073)). Three of these are this semester's coursework. Fall 2026 Project 2 covers Burt–Adelson stacks and per-band seams ([fa26 proj2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cal-cs180/cal-cs180.github.io/main/fa26/hw/proj2/index.html)). Project 3 asks for SDEdit-style edits at "starting time of [0.02,0.04,0.06,0.10,0.14,0.25,0.5]" ([fa26 proj3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cal-cs180/cal-cs180.github.io/main/fa26/hw/proj3-flow/parta.html)). Project 4 is homographies and mosaics ([fa26 proj4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cal-cs180/cal-cs180.github.io/main/fa26/hw/proj3/index.html)). The mosaic spec even carries the hint behind the Grow blend: "If your only problem is 'ghosting' of high-frequency terms, then a 2-level pyramid should be enough" ([fa25 proj3 part A](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cal-cs180/fa25/main/hw/proj3/partA.html)). The human contribution sits on top: the crossing rules, the foot-depth rule, the Grow ladder morph, the seam test with sub-pixel registration, and the kiosk.

### What the four measured numbers actually prove

The seam result is the project's best quantitative card, but it must be described exactly. The 3–4 px "before" numbers come from a **synthetic drift injected on purpose** (`--perturb`) into filtered styles, to test the registration. They are not drift produced by the generator. After homography plus a local warp, the residual is **0.13–0.53 px** on 7 of 9 styles, inside the "<2 px" goal. The two sparse styles (ink, neon) fall back to 1.2–2.3 px. Separately, all 7 Stable Diffusion worlds passed the alignment check unwarped: ControlNet held the geometry. So the honest wording is "the seam catches drift, and registration removes it", not "the generator drifts 4 px". A local warp fitted to the same edges it is scored on can also flatter itself, which is why the gap table asks for held-out edges. Cite DINO structure distance as the standard alternative and explain the choice. ReFlex notes that DINO structure distance "can be unreliable when there are large semantic changes, such as big shifts in color distribution" ([arXiv 2507.01496](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01496)), and night and underwater worlds are exactly such shifts.

The **35 dB PSNR** identity check shows the compositing path (mask, ratio shadows, plate) reproduces the footage when the world is the plate itself. Most pixels in that frame are background, where plate and footage already agree up to sensor noise, so a whole-frame PSNR is dominated by easy pixels. Report it inside person masks and shadow bands as well, and against a plain alpha composite without the ratio term, before it goes on a slide.

The **r = 0.80** correlation between person height and foot-point depth over **4,154 detections** supports the C3 rule. Monocular depth at the ground contact agrees with apparent size, as single-view geometry predicts, so assigning each person one depth is reasonable. The squared correlation, about 0.64, leaves a third of the variance to real height differences, sitting, cropping and depth error. Say whether "depth" here is Depth Anything V2's relative inverse depth, because pixel height should scale with inverse depth. A fixed camera looking at a ground plane also predicts height from the foot's image row alone, as Hoiem et al. exploit, so the decisive test is whether depth beats that free baseline.

The **80 s per world** figure is the cleanest systems argument once it is set beside per-frame regeneration. Running the same CPU pipeline on every frame of a one-minute 30 fps clip would take 1,800 × 80 s, about **40 hours**, against 80 s once. That is the team's own arithmetic, not a measurement. The real-time industry closes that gap with hardware instead: StreamDiffusionV2 uses four H100s for about 58 fps on a 14B model, and Krea uses a B200 for 11 fps ([StreamDiffusionV2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07399v2); [Krea](https://huggingface.co/krea/krea-realtime-video)). The price of generating once is that the world is static and cannot react to the scene except through compositing. A moved chair stays wrong until the world is regenerated.

## One idea, pitched three ways

### Grandparent

"We put a camera on a tripod at Sather Gate and filmed people walking by. Because the camera never moves, we can work out what the empty plaza looks like and take everyone out. We ask an AI to repaint that one empty picture as a snowy night, or as the 1920s. Then we put the real people back in. Now draw a line down the middle: on one side it's today, on the other it's the painting. When someone walks across, they step from our world into the other one. Because we only repainted one picture, the painted world stays perfectly still instead of shimmering like most AI video."

### Hiring manager

"Generative video today regenerates every pixel of every frame. Real-time systems need H100- or B200-class GPUs, and editors like Runway Aleph cap clips at 30 seconds and still let the scene drift. We asked what you get if you move all the generation offline. With a fixed camera, a temporal median gives a clean plate. We restyle it once per world, about 80 seconds on four CPU cores with Stable Diffusion 1.5, ControlNet and LCM. After that the live path is only masking and a shader. We register the generated plate back to the real one with a homography and a local warp, which cuts edge misalignment from about 4 px to under 0.6 px. That alignment lets us show reality and the generated world in one frame, split by a line, a depth threshold or a portal, so people visibly cross between them. Our own design choices are the crossing rules, a foot-depth rule validated at r = 0.80 over 4,154 detections, and a morph that reuses the model's partial generations. We give up camera motion and a world that reacts to the scene. In exchange the world never flickers, the real people stay real, and it runs on a laptop."

### Paper (abstract-length)

"We present Cross the Line, a system that splits a static-camera video into real and generated regions which viewers and subjects can cross. Building on restyle-once compositing [Teams Decorate; FXjutsu] and atlas-based video editing [Kasten et al. 2021; Ouyang et al. 2024], we observe that a locked-off camera reduces the background atlas to a clean plate recoverable by a per-pixel temporal median. We restyle that plate once per world with a depth- and edge-conditioned diffusion model, check its registration against the plate (ControlNet kept all 7 worlds aligned; an injected 3–4 px drift is reduced to 0.13–0.53 px by homography and local warping), and composite the original people back with Chuang-style ratio shadows (35 dB PSNR on the identity composite). A spatial rule (a line, a depth threshold, or a portal) chooses per pixel between the real frame and the restyled plate. A foot-contact depth rule (r = 0.80 with person height, n = 4,154) assigns each person to one world, so a body is never split. A two-band temporal blend over SDEdit strength rungs yields a transition with exact endpoints. Because the generated region is a single image, it is temporally constant by construction; the same design runs as a live kiosk at camera rate on a laptop."

## A related-work section ready for the 280A paper

The draft below is written to paste. Bracketed citations map to the links given. Before submission, recheck the items flagged in the gap table as snippet-only.

> **Related Work**
>
> **Diffusion-based video restyling.** Text-to-image diffusion with spatial conditioning [ControlNet; Zhang et al. 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2023/html/Zhang_Adding_Conditional_Control_to_Text-to-Image_Diffusion_Models_ICCV_2023_paper.html) made per-frame video editing straightforward but temporally unstable. Zero-shot methods add consistency through cross-frame attention and feature propagation [Pix2Video; Ceylan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12688), [TokenFlow; Geyer et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.10373), inversion-feature fusion [FateZero; Qi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09535), token merging [VidToMe; Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10656), or keyframe translation with patch-based propagation [Rerender-A-Video; Yang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07954), [FRESCO; Yang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12962). FRESCO identifies the shared failure: correspondence constraints are soft, optical flow fails under occlusion and fast motion, and frame-by-frame generation accumulates error. Recent stylisers built on video diffusion, [FreeViS; Xu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01686) and [VISTA; Song et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17312), and commercial and open video-to-video editors such as Runway Aleph and [VACE; Jiang et al. 2025](https://github.com/ali-vilab/VACE), improve coherence but regenerate every pixel and are limited to clips of roughly 5–30 s; minute-scale editing must be segmented, which introduces structural drift and boundary artifacts [MLV-Edit; Cao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02123). Real-time streaming systems [StreamDiffusion; Kodaira et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12491v2), [StreamV2V; Liang et al. 2025](https://jeff-liangf.github.io/projects/streamv2v), [StreamDiffusionV2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07399) reach interactive rates on high-end GPUs while still regenerating the subject. We regenerate no pixel per frame: the generated content is one image per world.
>
> **Canonical and layered video representations.** [Layered Neural Atlases; Kasten et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11418) map a video to 2D atlases so that a single edit propagates to all frames; [Text2LIVE; Bar-Tal et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02491) and [StableVideo; Chai et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09592) edit such atlases with CLIP or diffusion guidance. [CoDeF; Ouyang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07926), [Hashing-NVD; Chan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14022) and [NaRCan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06523) reduce optimisation time and model lighting change, for example through multiplicative residuals. Omnimatte methods [Lu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06993), [Lee et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16683) decompose video into a background and per-subject layers that carry shadows and reflections. All of these optimise per video to support camera motion. We observe that with a locked-off camera and a static scene, the background mapping is constant in time, so the background atlas reduces to a clean plate that a per-pixel temporal median recovers without optimisation. We give up camera motion and foreground editing in exchange for a closed-form, instantaneous canonical image.
>
> **Background estimation, matting and the clean plate.** The temporal median is a standard background model [Piccardi 2004](https://zenodo.org/records/891536/files/article.pdf), and differencing against a captured background underlies [Background Matting; Sengupta et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00626) and its real-time successor [Lin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07810). In visual-effects practice a locked-off camera makes the clean plate easy to obtain [Foundry](https://learn.foundry.com/nuke/8.0/content/furnacecore/background.html), and matte painting replaces the environment behind rotoscoped actors; our pipeline automates the plate, the matte and the painting. [Video Synopsis; Rav-Acha et al. 2006](https://vision.huji.ac.il/synopsis/cvpr06-synopsis.pdf) similarly re-composites moving objects over a stationary-camera background, without changing the background's appearance.
>
> **Generate-once compositing.** Video-conferencing products generate a background once and composite the segmented user every frame: Zoom and Google Meet from a text prompt, and Microsoft Teams Premium from a restyled snapshot of the user's own room [Microsoft 2024](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/microsoft-teams-blog/decorate-your-background-in-microsoft-teams-premium-now/ba-p/4042705). The open-source FXjutsu "Toon World" preset restyles an empty video plate and overlays the real subjects [FXjutsu 2026](https://github.com/audriu/fxjutsu). These systems replace the entire background. Our contribution is to keep a pixel-aligned real version alongside the generated one and let a spatial rule choose between them.
>
> **Spatial boundaries between versions of a scene.** Joining multiple versions of one fixed view along a seam has a long history in photography and graphics. Stephen Wilkes's *Day to Night* composites about 1,500 exposures so that time runs across the frame [NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/alltechconsidered/2016/02/19/467338386/when-people-can-see-time-photographer-captures-day-night-in-one-image); [DuctTake; Rüegg et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.04772) composites takes of one scene along spatiotemporal seams; AR portals reveal an authored 3D world through a doorway [Snap Lens Studio](https://developers.snap.com/lens-studio/examples/lens-examples/portal); and multiresolution blending joins images along irregular masks [Burt and Adelson 1983](https://persci.mit.edu/pub_abstracts/spline83_abs.html). We are not aware of prior work in which the far side of such a boundary is a generated restyle of the same live or filmed scene and people change world by crossing it. For depth-based splits we assign each person the depth at their ground-contact point, following the single-view reasoning of [Hoiem et al. 2008](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/2008/10/ijcv2008ObjectsInPerspective.pdf) and [Lalonde et al. 2007](https://hci.iwr.uni-heidelberg.de/vislearn/HTML/publications/papers/2007/criminisi_siggraph_07.pdf).
>
> **Shadows and harmonisation.** We transfer shadows as the ratio of each frame to the clean plate, a simple instance of [Shadow Matting and Compositing; Chuang et al. 2003](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/digital-matting/shadow-matting/), and match the people's colour to each world with one global transform, in the spirit of domain-matching harmonisation [DoveNet; Cong et al. 2020](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/html/Cong_DoveNet_Deep_Image_Harmonization_via_Domain_Verification_CVPR_2020_paper.html). Learned relighting [IC-Light; Zhang et al. 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1cce374fec7829c24a97ae0e91cd0999-Paper-Conference.pdf), [ZeroComp; Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08168) can change the subject's shading but jitters when applied per frame [Light-A-Video](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08590); our global transform cannot jitter but cannot relight.
>
> **Transitions and morphing.** Diffusion morphing interpolates between two images through LoRA weights, latents or attention [DiffMorpher; Zhang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07409), [IMPUS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06792), [FreeMorph; Cao et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01953), and naive low-noise interpolation resembles an alpha composite [Wang and Golland 2023](https://clintonjwang.github.io/interpolation). [Differential Diffusion; Levin and Fried 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00950v2) varies edit strength per pixel within one generation, and community tools animate denoising strength with frame feedback [Loopback Wave](https://rentry.co/sd-loopback-wave). Our "Grow" transition instead plays the cached strength ladder of a single SDEdit generation [Meng et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.01073) from plate to world, blending neighbouring rungs with [Brown and Lowe's](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/lci/papers/docs2003/brown03.pdf) two-band rule applied over time: low frequencies crossfade, while high frequencies switch at a per-pixel threshold so that fine detail is never shown from two rungs at once.
>
> **Evaluating geometric fidelity.** Structure preservation in image editing is usually scored with DINO self-similarity structure distance [Tumanyan et al. 2022], as adopted by the PIE-Bench protocol [Ju et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01506), which is known to be unreliable under large colour changes [ReFlex](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01496). Because one side of our boundary is untouched, we instead report the edge displacement across the seam in pixels, a registration error that stays interpretable for night or underwater worlds.

The DuctTake author line (Rüegg et al.), the Tumanyan et al. 2022 origin of DINO structure distance and the Ju et al. attribution of PIE-Bench come from background knowledge and should be checked against the primary entries.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the project's value sits. The restyle-once pipeline, the part that looks most like the method, is the most thoroughly anticipated: a meetings product, an open-source preset and a 2021 SIGGRAPH Asia paper each own a version of it. The part that looks most like art direction, a boundary people walk across between reality and its generated twin, is the part with no precedent found. The order matters for the talk. Lead with the crossing, then explain that a tripod collapses atlas editing to a median, and that this collapse is what makes an aligned twin cheap enough to run live. Under that framing every borrowed component becomes evidence of fluency rather than a dent in novelty. The course's own FAQ rewards a technically simple, creative project, so this is the strongest position available, not a retreat.

The second implication is that the team's best evidence is its own error budget, not a comparison with others. The 4 px drift its generator produced, and the registration that removed it, demonstrate that the line is self-checking in a way no Aleph-style editor documents. One afternoon of per-frame baseline runs turns "cannot flicker" from an argument into a figure. Three cheap controls (held-out seam edges, the foot-row baseline and masked PSNR) then make each measured number hard to argue with.
